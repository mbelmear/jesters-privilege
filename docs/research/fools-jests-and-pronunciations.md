# Fools' jests, facts, and pronunciations

Research for #120. It is the source material for the Fools' Gallery rewrite (#123): the best-attested lines of the seven Fools with jests, a checked sentence or two on who each one was, pronunciations for the hard names, and a list of what the current stories (`src/content/gallery.ts`, `src/content/home.ts`) claim that the sources don't support.

## Method

Each claim was traced back to the earliest source I could read, with digitised primary texts preferred: Internet Archive scans, EEBO-TCP transcriptions, Wikisource, British History Online, and Project Gutenberg. Where I only found a primary source quoted inside a later book, the citation says so. I could not read Otto's _Fools Are Everywhere_, Welsford's _The Fool_, Southworth's _Fools and Jesters at the English Court_, Ivanov's _Holy Fools in Byzantium and Beyond_, the Oxford DNB, or the _Polski Słownik Biograficzny_. For the English Fools I used the original _Dictionary of National Biography_ (1885–1900) instead, and for Stańczyk the Polish primary texts and a summary of Małgorzata Wilska's work. Merriam-Webster and Collins blocked automated reading, so the pronunciations rest on Wiktionary's IPA and the languages' stress rules (details under Pronunciations).

**Labels**

- **Attested**: written down during the Fool's lifetime or soon after, by someone in a position to know. The entry gives the gap between the event and the record.
- **Legend**: a later anecdote, or one with no contemporary record. The Gallery introduces these with "the story goes".

Jests drift between Fools, and the specialists warn about it. Canel (1873) calls the Triboulet "quarter of an hour" quip "a witticism spread under a crowd of different names"[9], and Doran (1858) says something like it "is told of at least half-a-dozen wearers of motley"[12]. So each line below is dated by the earliest place I actually found it.

## Stańczyk

**Who he was.** Stańczyk was the fool of the Polish kings Alexander and Sigismund I, and possibly of Sigismund Augustus; the chronicler Marcin Bielski (1495–1575) called him "an exceptional fool" whose jokes told the truth[1][3]. He is recorded alive in 1556, and Mikołaj Rej writes of him as dead in 1562[7]. Wilska, the author of his _Polski Słownik Biograficzny_ entry, puts his birth around 1470 and his death most likely in 1557 or 1558[6].

### Candidate lines

**1. The bear (Attested: Bielski, a contemporary of the court; printed 1597).**

> "Większy to błazen, co mając niedźwiedzia w skrzyni, puszcza go na swoją szkodę."

_He's the bigger fool who has a bear in a crate and lets it out to his own harm._

- **Setup.** A huge bear was brought from Lithuania in a crate (_skrzynia_) to be baited at Niepołomice, with the king, Queen Bona, and the court watching on horseback. The bear mauled the dogs and charged the royal party. The queen fell from her horse while pregnant and lost the child, and the bear knocked Stańczyk over, horse and all. The king laughed at him: "Począłeś sobie nie jako rycerz, ale jako błazen, żeś przed niedźwiedziem uciekał" (_You carried on not like a knight but like a fool, running from the bear_)[1].
- **Sources.** Bielski, _Kronika polska_, book V[1]. Bielski's narrative sets it just after the Kraków sejm that ran into 1532[1], Kraszewski dates it 1533[2], Gloger 1522[3], and English Wikipedia 1527[7]. Leave the year out.
- **Note.** In Bielski the king laughs rather than scolds, and the bear comes out of a crate rather than a cage. Historians read the line as a dig at the king's leniency toward Prussia, but English Wikipedia flags that reading as uncited[7]. Bielski himself draws the moral: "with fools it is a mistake for lords to joke, for they like to tell the truth in jest"[1].

**2. Smolensk (Attested: Bielski, printed 1597).**

> "Barziej królu ciebie drą a niż mnie, anoć wydarto Smoleńsk a przecie milczysz."

_They're skinning you worse than me, king: Smolensk has been torn away from you, and you say nothing._

- **Setup.** Some boys set on Stańczyk and tore the clothes off him. He ran to the king, who pitied him[1].
- **Sources.** Bielski, immediately after the bear story[1]. The loss of Smolensk is the news on the letter in Matejko's painting.
- **Note.** This is the strongest line for the Gallery: it is in the earliest source, it is about truth-telling, and it ties to the painting the show is named after.

**3. The two liars (Attested: Jan Kochanowski, a younger contemporary; printed 1590).**

> "Stańczyk powiadał, że niemasz więtszych łgarzów w Polszcze, jeno arcybiskup Gamrat, a Maciejowski biskup krakowski; bo ów powiadał: wszytko wiem — a nie wiedział nic; ten zaś mówił: rad, wierę, nie wiem — a wszytko wiedział."

_Stańczyk used to say there were no bigger liars in Poland than Archbishop Gamrat and Maciejowski, Bishop of Kraków: the first always said "I know everything" and knew nothing, and the second said "I really don't know" and knew everything._

- **Setup.** Two bishops of Sigismund I's court, Piotr Gamrat and Samuel Maciejowski[4].
- **Sources.** Kochanowski, _Apophthegmata_, published after his death in Januszowski's edition of his _Fragmenta_[4].

**4. The toothache bet (Legend: no record before the 19th century).**

- **Setup.** At a dinner the talk turned to which trade has the most practitioners. Stańczyk said doctors and offered to show a hundred of them in Kraków within three days. A lord took the bet for 100 złoty. Stańczyk tied up his jaw and walked the town complaining of toothache, writing down everyone who prescribed a cure, the lord himself included. When the lord protested, "Albom ci ja lekarz?" (_Am I a doctor, then?_), Stańczyk answered, "Przecieżeś mi wasz mość na zęby lekarstwa nauczył" (_But your lordship taught me a cure for toothache_)[2].
- **Sources.** The earliest version I found is Kraszewski's 1839 biography[2][7]. Kraszewski footnotes it to Górnicki's _Dworzanin polski_ (1566), but the full Wikisource text of _Dworzanin_ names Stańczyk nowhere and contains no such bet[5]. Matejko painted _Stańczyk Faking a Toothache_ in 1856. Google Arts & Culture tells the story with the bet placed with King Sigismund I and cites no source[8].
- **Note.** In Kraszewski the bet is with an unnamed lord, not the king. The current Gallery wording, a bet "over which trade had the most practitioners", fits Kraszewski, but it needs "the story goes".

**Also usable:** Górnicki records Stańczyk asking Sigismund I in 1539 why he was sending his daughter Isabella to Hungary, and telling him to build her a house in Kraków to come back to. She did come back, widowed. This comes from Górnicki's _Dzieje w Koronie Polskiej_ as quoted by Kraszewski and National Geographic Polska; I did not read Górnicki's text[2][6].

## Triboulet

**Who he was.** Triboulet was the household fool of Louis XII and Francis I. The poet Jean Marot, who knew him, described him in 1509 as "a fool with a cracked head, as wise at thirty as the day he was born" ("Triboulet fut un fol de la teste escorné, / Aussi saige à trente ans que le jour qu'il fut né")[9][10]. In other words, he was a "natural" fool, kept for his simplicity, not a wit. Scholars think there were at least two fools called Triboulet, later merged into one. He died in 1536 or 1537, depending on the source[10][13].

### Candidate lines

**1. The horse that won't stop (Attested: Bonaventure des Périers, a near-contemporary; printed 1558).**

> "Par le sang Dieu, disoit Triboulet, … ce meschant cheval, je le picque tant que je puis, encores ne veult-il pas demeurer !"

_"By God's blood," said Triboulet, "this wicked horse! I spur it as hard as I can, and it still won't stand still!"_

- **Setup.** At a royal entry into Rouen, Triboulet was sent ahead on a fine caparisoned horse to announce the king. His keeper shouted at him to stop. Triboulet, afraid of a beating, pulled at the reins while driving his spurs in as hard as he could[10].
- **Sources.** Des Périers, _Nouvelles récréations et joyeux devis_, nouvelle II[10]. The editor dates the entry to Louis XII's at Rouen, 28 September 1508[10].
- **Note.** This fits the Triboulet the sources describe: he is funny because he doesn't understand, not because he is clever.

**2. The noisy bishop (Legend: Canel cites it to the Des Périers collection of 1558, but I couldn't find it there, so it isn't confirmed as a near-contemporary record).**

> "Da, da, mon cousin, quand nous sommes entrés céans, il n'y avoit point de bruit, et cestui-ci a commencé la noise : c'est donc lui qu'il faut punir."

_Well, cousin, when we came in here it was quiet, and this fellow started the racket, so he's the one to punish._

- **Setup.** At vespers in the Sainte-Chapelle, a bishop intoned _Deus in adjutorium_ and the choir burst in. Triboulet went up to the bishop and punched him. Asked by the king why, he gave this answer[9].
- **Sources.** Canel quotes it from Des Périers, "t. II, p. 167"[9]; I didn't find it in the nouvelle II text I read. Canel adds that Lodovico Domenichi soon told the same story in an Italian joke book without naming its hero[9].
- **Note.** For a show that pokes fun at church culture, this is the best Triboulet line, but it still needs "the story goes".

**3. "Beware the monk" (Legend: a scene in Rabelais's novel, the _Tiers Livre_, 1546, about ten years after his death; the book is fiction, not a record, though it shows how he was remembered).**

> "Par Dieu, Dieu, fol enraigé, guare moine, cornemuse de Buzançay !"

_By God, God, raving fool, beware the monk, bagpipe of Buzançais!_

- **Setup.** Panurge cannot decide whether to marry. He has consulted a theologian (ch. 30), a physician (chs. 31–34), and a philosopher (chs. 35–36). Pantagruel then persuades him to take counsel of a fool (ch. 37). Triboulet arrives by water from Blois, drinks Panurge's wine, punches him between the shoulders, flicks his nose with a pig's bladder, says only this, and refuses to say anything more[11]. Canel reports that "Buzançois" was a word Triboulet really used all the time[9].
- **Sources.** Rabelais, _Tiers Livre_, chs. 37, 38, and 45[11].

**4. "A quarter of an hour before" (Legend: a wandering jest).**

> "Ah ! cousin, … grand merci vous dirois, s'il vous agrée plutôt de le faire pendre un quart d'heure avant."

_Thank you kindly, cousin, but would you be so good as to hang him a quarter of an hour before?_

- **Setup.** A great lord threatened to have Triboulet beaten to death for talking about him too freely. The king promised to hang the man a quarter of an hour after Triboulet's death[9].
- **Sources.** The earliest version I found is Canel (1873)[9], with the same story in Doran (1858)[12]. Both men disown it: Canel calls it "a witticism spread under a crowd of different names" that could only have reached Triboulet's mouth "by way of a reminiscence"[9]. Doran says the best things attributed to Triboulet "are of questionable authority" and that this one is "told of at least half-a-dozen wearers of motley"[12]. I found no 16th-century source.
- **Note.** If the Gallery keeps it, it must be framed as legend, and it should not be the title of his story.

**Drift between Fools.** Triboulet is also credited with offering Francis I his fool's cap if Charles V was fool enough to cross France. Doran dismisses it: "Triboulet was in his grave before the last delicate affair was even negotiated (1538)"[12]. The same "I'll give the cap to the other king" joke is told of Archibald Armstrong and Prince Charles's trip to Spain, and Doran calls that version "very apocryphal" too[12].

## Will Somers

**Who he was.** William Somer (Somers, Sommers) was Henry VIII's fool. He first appears in the royal accounts on 28 June 1535, he went on to serve Edward VI and Mary, and he was probably the William Sommers buried at St Leonard's, Shoreditch, on 15 June 1560[17]. The comic actor Robert Armin, writing in 1608 when, as he says, "so many knew him", remembered him as lean, hollow-eyed, and stooping, "a poor man's friend", whose "merry prate kept with the King much rule. / When he was sad the King with him would rhyme; / Thus Will exilëd sadness many a time"[16].

### Candidate lines

**1. "Frauditors" (Attested: Thomas Wilson, _The Arte of Rhetorique_, 1553, printed while Somers was alive).**

> "William Somer seyng muche a do for accomptes makyng, & that the Kynges Maiestie of most worthie memorie Henry theight wanted mony suche as was due vnto hym: And please your grace (quod he) you haue so many frauditours, so many conueiers, and so many deceiuers to get vp your money, that they get al to themselues."

_Seeing a great fuss over the accounts, and that Henry VIII was short of money owed to him, Somers said: "If it please your grace, you have so many frauditors, so many conveyers, and so many deceivers to collect your money that they keep it all for themselves."_

- **Setup.** Wilson gives it as his example of "altering part of a word". Somers "should have said auditors, surveyors, and receivers", Wilson explains, adding: "it was vnhappely spoken of a foole, & I thynke he had some Scholemaister"[15].
- **Sources.** Wilson 1553 (STC 25799)[15]. The passage is unchanged in the 1560 edition[15].
- **Note.** The current story says Somers spoke of "so many auditors … and so many 'frauditors'". In fact he made three puns, one for each kind of official, and never said "auditors" at all. Wilson's phrase "of most worthie memorie" shows the scene took place under Henry VIII and was written down after his death in 1547.

**2. The cardinal's debts (Legend: Armin, 1608, about 80 years after the scene).**

> "To whom dost thou owe thy soule? To God, quoth hee. To whom thy wealth? To the poore, sayes hee. Take thy forfeit (Harry) sayes the foole; … to the poore at the gate I paid his debt, which hee yeelds is due."

- **Setup.** With Henry dining at Cardinal Wolsey's, Somers asked the king to lend him £10 to pay "three or four of the cardinal's creditors". Wolsey swore on his head that he owed no one a penny and lent Will the money himself. Will gave it all to the poor waiting at the gate, then made Wolsey admit that he owed his wealth to the poor[16].
- **Sources.** Armin, _A Nest of Ninnies_ (1608)[16]. Wolsey died in 1530, and Somers is first recorded at court in 1535[17], so Armin's story probably borrows Somers's name for an older joke.

**3. Three riddles for a melancholy king (Legend: Armin, 1608).**

> "Harry, sayes hee, what is it, that the lesser it is, the more it is to be feared? … it was a little bridge ouer a deepe riuer."

- **Setup.** Henry was so low that nothing Will did could cheer him. Will went behind the arras to "study three questions" and came back with three riddles. The second is that a ditch-dauber has the cleanest trade, "for then he washes him cleane againe". The third is bawdy. The king "laught hartely"[16].
- **Sources.** Armin (1608), who insists "that Will Sommers asked them of the king, it is certaine: there are some will affirme it now living at Greenwich"[16].

**Note on the psalter.** In Henry VIII's psalter (British Library, Royal MS 2 A XVI, f. 63v), Henry is painted as David with his harp and Somers stands beside him, at Psalm 52[18]. In the Vulgate's numbering, Psalm 52 begins _Dixit insipiens in corde suo_ ("The fool hath said in his heart"); it is Psalm 53 in English Bibles.

## Richard Tarlton

**Who he was.** Richard Tarlton (d. 1588) was the most famous comic actor of Elizabethan England and one of the twelve players chosen for the new Queen's Men in 1583. He was celebrated for making up verses on the spot from themes the audience shouted out, and Nashe wrote that "the people began exceedingly to laugh when Tarlton first peept out his head"[21]. The Dictionary of National Biography says that "in spite of royal patronage and popular appreciation he was poor". He died in Shoreditch in September 1588 and was buried at St Leonard's[19][21].

### Candidate lines

**1. "Take away the knave" (Attested: a court note of about August 1588, from Tarlton's own lifetime).**

> "How Tarlton played the God Luz with a flitch of bacon at his back, and how the Queen bade them take away the knave for making her to laugh so excessively, as he fought against her little dog, Perrico de Faldas, with his sword and long staffe, and bade the Queen take off her mastie."

- **Setup.** Tarlton, who held the rank of master of fence[21], duelled the queen's lapdog with sword and staff as if it were a fighting mastiff, and called on her to "take off" her "mastiff". She laughed so hard she had him removed[19].
- **Sources.** A scrap of paper among the State Papers Domestic (vol. CCXV, no. 89, dated c. August 1588), calendared in 1865[19].
- **Note.** This is the real source for "she had him taken away for making her laugh too much". The joke was about the dog, not about Raleigh and Leicester (see line 2).

**2. "See, the knave commands the Queen" (Legend: Edmund Bohun, _The Character of Queen Elizabeth_, 1693, about 105 years later).**

> "When it was acting before the Queen, he pointed at Sir Walter Raleigh, and said, 'See, the Knave commands the Queen!' — for which he was corrected by a frown from the Queen."

- **Setup.** Tarlton went on to attack Raleigh's "too intolerable a power" and the "over-great power and riches of the Earl of Leicester". The court applauded, and the queen hid her displeasure, "but yet she was so offended that she forbade Tarleton and all her jesters from coming near her table"[12][20].
- **Sources.** Bohun (1693), as quoted by Doran and Halliwell[12][20]. The pun works because _knave_ was also the name of a playing card, so the knave "commanding" the queen sounds like a card game.
- **Note.** In this story Elizabeth is offended, not laughing.

**3. Small beer (Legend: _Tarlton's Jests_; first part probably c. 1592, earliest surviving edition 1611).**

> "Her Majestie … commanded that he should have no more; for, quoth shee, he will play the beast, and so shame himselfe. Feare not you, quoth Tarlton, for your beere is small enough. Whereat Her Majestie laughed heartily, and commanded that he should have enough."

- **Setup.** To lift the queen out of a bad mood, Tarlton pretended to be drunk and called for beer. _Small beer_ is weak beer, so he is joking about the palace's stingy ale[20].
- **Sources.** _Tarlton's Jests_, the first of the "Court-witty Jests"[20]. The Dictionary of National Biography calls the book "more or less fictitious anecdotes", many of them "far older than Tarlton"[21].

**4. The sculler (Legend: John Taylor, _Wit and Mirth_, 1630).**

> "Dicke Tarleton said that hee would compare Queene Elizabeth to nothing more fitly then to a sculler, for, said he, neither the Queene nor the sculler hath a fellow."

- **Setup.** A sculler rows a boat alone, with no partner ("fellow") at the other oars. The queen likewise has no equal, and no husband[20].
- **Sources.** Taylor (1630), quoted by Halliwell[20].

**Fuller's verdict (Legend: Fuller, 1662, 74 years after Tarlton's death; a summing-up, not a jest).** "He told the Queen more of her faults than most of her chaplains, and cured her melancholy better than all of her physicians." This comes from Thomas Fuller, _Worthies of England_ (1662), which says Tarlton was born at Condover, Shropshire. I read Fuller's words as quoted by Halliwell[20][21].

## Archibald Armstrong

**Who he was.** Archibald "Archy" Armstrong (d. 1672) was the fool of James VI and I and of Charles I. "According to a well-known tradition" he had first been a sheep-stealer on the Scottish border[22]. He went with Prince Charles to Madrid in 1623, grew rich, and was put out of court in March 1638 for insulting Archbishop Laud. He was buried at Arthuret, Cumberland, on 1 April 1672[22][23].

### Candidate lines

**1. "Who's fool now?" (Attested: a Privy Council order of the same day; the words printed by John Rushworth, a contemporary, in 1680).**

> "Whea's feule now? doth not your Grace hear the News from Striveling about the Liturgy?"

- **Setup.** News reached Whitehall that the king's proclamation backing the new Scottish prayer book had been read at Stirling (Striveling) in early March. On 11 March, Archy said this to Laud as the archbishop was going into the Council. It was "presently complained of to the Council", and the same day the king and Council ordered that "Archibald Armestrong, the Kings Fool, for certain scandalous words of a high nature, spoken by him against the Lord Arch-Bishop of Canterbury … shall have his Coat pulled over his head, and be discharged of the Kings Service, and banished the Court … And immediately the same was put in Execution"[23].
- **Sources.** Rushworth, _Historical Collections_, vol. 2, p. 471, under "At White-Hall the 11th of March, 1637"[23]. The English year then began on 25 March, so this is 11 March 1638 in modern dating. The Stirling proclamation and the order belong to February and March 1638.
- **Note.** The jibe came on the day of his dismissal and was about the Stirling news, months after the Edinburgh prayer-book riot of 1637. The current story compresses the two.

**2. "Little Laud to the devil" (Legend: earliest found 1693, more than 50 years later).**

> "Great praise be given to GOD, & litle laud to the devil."

- **Setup.** Edmund Hickeringill tells it this way: at Archy's request, Charles I let him say grace, "his Grace little Dr. Laud in Presence"[24]. A 1745 jest book has the king order Archy to say grace because the chaplain was missing, and explains that it "reflected upon Laud … who was a little Man"[25].
- **Sources.** Hickeringill, _The Ceremony-Monger_ (the archive.org copy is catalogued 1693)[24]; _The Merry Medley_ (1745)[25]; Doran (1858) introduces it with "It is even said"[12]; DNB 1885[22]. A full-text search of the Internet Archive found nothing earlier than 1693[39]. The 1630 _Banquet of Jeasts_, the jest book later sold under Archy's name, does not contain it and never names Archy; its preface is signed "Anonimos"[26].
- **Note.** The current story says Archy was "asked to say grace at a dinner Laud attended". Hickeringill, the earliest source, says Archy asked to say it. The line can stay, but it needs "the story goes".

**3. The Armada, told to the Spanish court (Attested: James Howell's letter dated Madrid, 1623; printed 1645).**

> "Was it not a strange thing, quoth he, that in the year '88 there should come a fleet of 140 sail from Spain to invade England, and that ten of these could not go back to tell what became of the rest?"

- **Setup.** Howell writes: "Our cousin Archy hath more privilege than any, for he often goes with his fool's coat where the Infanta is with her meninas and ladies of honour, and keeps a-blowing and blustering amongst them, and flurts out what he list." The Spanish ladies were marvelling at the Catholic victory at Prague, and Archy offered them "a stranger thing than that"[27].
- **Sources.** Howell, _Epistolae Ho-Elianae_, first published 1645[27][28].
- **Note.** The current story's "a courtier wrote home that Archy had more privilege than any of them" paraphrases this letter, which comes from Howell himself.

**4. Buckingham (Attested by a Spanish contemporary; I read it only through the DNB).**

> "No one has ever heard of a fool being threatened for talking, but many dukes have been beheaded for their insolence."

- **Setup.** In Madrid, Archy blamed the Duke of Buckingham for how the marriage negotiations had been handled, and Buckingham threatened to have him hanged[22].
- **Sources.** Fray Francisco de Jesús, _Narrative of the Spanish Marriage Treaty_ (Camden Society, p. 252), as quoted in the DNB[22]. A full-text search shows the ODNB repeats the same quotation[40]. I did not read the Spanish narrative itself.

## Folly, in Erasmus's _In Praise of Folly_

**Who she is.** Folly (Latin _Stultitia_, Greek _Moria_) is the speaker of Erasmus's _Moriae Encomium_, first printed in 1511. The book is a declamation, a mock speech that Folly delivers in her own praise to a "full assembly"[29][31]. In the prefatory letter Erasmus says he thought of it while riding from Italy to England, and that the title puns on his friend Thomas More's name: "your surname of More, which comes so near the word _Moriae_ (folly) as you are far from the thing"[30]. Wikipedia reports Erasmus's own claim that he wrote the first version in a week at More's house; I did not read Erasmus's letter saying so[31].

### Candidate lines

All of these are **Attested**: they are the text itself (1511). The English is John Wilson's 1668 translation[30] and the Latin is from The Latin Library[29].

**1. The entrance.**

> "…simulatque in hunc coetum frequentissimum dictura prodii, sic repente omnium uultus noua quadam atque insolita hilaritate enituerunt…"

_"…I no sooner stepped up to speak to this full assembly than all your faces put on a kind of new and unwonted pleasantness."_

- **Setup.** This is Folly's opening: her proof that she is a goddess is that everyone cheered up the moment she appeared[29][30].

**2. Popes.**

> "Iam Summi Pontifices, qui Christi vices gerunt, si conentur eiusdem vitam aemulari, nempe paupertatem, labores, doctrinam, crucem, vitae contemptum … quid erit in terris afflictius?"

_"And for popes, that supply the place of Christ, if they should endeavor to imitate His life, to wit His poverty, labor, doctrine, cross, and contempt of life … who would live more disconsolate than themselves?"_

- **Setup.** This comes from Folly's tour of the powerful, after the theologians, monks, and bishops: if wisdom ever got into the papacy, it would lose all its wealth, honours, and "so many pardons"[29][30].

**3. Fools for Christ.**

> "Rursum alibi: 'Nos', inquit, 'stulti propter Christum.'"

_"…and in another place, 'We are fools for Christ's sake.'"_

- **Setup.** This is the turn at the end of the book. Folly piles up texts from Paul ("The foolishness of God is wiser than men", "God hath chosen the foolish things of this world") and ends by praising "the foolishness of the cross"[29][30].

**4. The exit.**

> "…memineritis illius Graecanici proverbii _pollaki toi kai môros anêr katakairion eipen_ … Quare valete, plaudite, vivite, bibite, Moriae celeberrimi Mystae."

_"…remembering in the meantime that Greek proverb, 'Sometimes a fool may speak a word in season' … Wherefore farewell, clap your hands, live and drink lustily, my most excellent disciples of Folly."_

- **Setup.** These are her last words. She excuses anything too bold as spoken by a fool and a woman, then reminds the audience that even a fool can say the right thing at the right time[29][30]. It is a natural motto for the show.

**Holbein.** In 1515, Oswald Myconius had Hans Holbein the Younger and his brother Ambrosius fill the margins of his copy of the Basel edition with pen drawings; the copy is now in the Kunstmuseum Basel. Most sources count 82 drawings, 79 by Hans and 3 by Ambrosius[32][33]. Erasmus's quip that if he still looked like his portrait "he could readily get a wife" is reported in a review of a scholarly edition[32]. I did not see Myconius's note itself.

## The yurodivy: Nikola of Pskov and Ivan the Terrible

**Who they were.** In Russia, the _yurodivy_ (holy fool) was a man who went "starke naked, save a clout about their middle … even in the very extremity of winter", and was taken "as prophets and men of great holines, giving them a liberty to speak what they list without any controulment, thogh it be of the very highest himselfe"[34]. So wrote the Englishman Giles Fletcher in 1591, describing what he had seen "at my beeing at Mosko"[34]. Nikola (Nicholas Salos) of Pskov died in 1576 and is venerated as a saint[36]. Basil (Vasily) the Blessed rebuked Ivan IV "for all his crueltie"; his body was moved to "a sumptuous church neere the emperours house in Mosko", and he was canonized[34].

### Candidate lines

**1. The raw meat (Attested: Fletcher, 1591, about 21 years after the event; Fletcher writes from his own stay in Moscow).**

> "And doth Ivasko (which is as much to saye, as Jacke) thinke … that it is unlawfull to eate a piece of beasts flesh in Lent, and not to eate up so much mans flesh as hee hath done already?"

- **Setup.** Ivan IV ("this emperours father", as Fletcher wrote in Tsar Feodor's reign) came to sack Pskov on suspicion of rebellion. He greeted the hermit Nikola and sent him a reward. Nikola sent back "a piece of rawe fleshe, beyng then their Lent time". Ivan had a messenger say he was surprised a holy man would offer him meat in Lent, against the Church's rule. Nikola gave this answer and threatened him with a prophecy of disaster unless he stopped the killing and left, and "he saved a great many mens lives at that time"[34].
- **Sources.** Fletcher, _Of the Russe Common Wealth_ (1591), in the chapter on their ecclesiastical state[34].
- **Note.** In Fletcher's version the exchange goes through a messenger, and Nikola speaks of eating "mans flesh", not of drinking "Christian blood". He also calls the tsar by the belittling _Ivashko_ ("Jack"), which is a joke in itself.

**2. "Eater of Christian flesh" (Attested: Jerome Horsey, an Englishman in Russia under Ivan, who says he saw Nikola himself; his manuscript was first printed in full in 1856).**

> "…a holly man, named Mickula Sweat [Mikula Svyatoy, 'Saint Mikula'], whoe, by his bold imprecacions and exsorsims, railings and threats, terminge him the Emperour bloudsuccer, the devourer and eater of Christian flesh, and swore by his angell that he should not escape deathe of a present thounder boltt, if he or any of his army did touch a hear in displeasur of the least childs head in that cittie…"

- **Setup.** Ivan arrived at Pskov to do there what he had done elsewhere. A storm was breaking at that very moment, and "These wordes made the Emperour to trembell". Horsey adds: "I saw this impostur or magicion, a fowll creature, went naked both in winter and sommer"[35].
- **Sources.** Horsey, _Travels_, in the same 1856 volume[35]. Horsey gets the order of events wrong: he puts Pskov after the siege of Reval (1577) and before Novgorod. The usual date for the Pskov confrontation is February 1570, after Novgorod[36].
- **Note.** Horsey is not friendly to the holy man ("impostur or magician"), yet he still records the rebuke and the tsar's fear.

**3. Bread and salt, not Christian blood (Legend: the saint's _Life_ and church tradition; date not established).**

> "Ivanushko, Ivanushko, eat our bread and salt, and not Christian blood."

- **Setup.** The people of Pskov met the tsar with bread and salt, and Nikola ran out to him with these words. Later versions add the raw meat, the line about drinking human blood, and the death of Ivan's best horse[36].
- **Sources.** This is the wording of the Orthodox calendar and _Life_ tradition, in English translation. I could not find it in a Pskov chronicle text, so treat it as hagiography[36].

**4. The Moscow fool against the Godunovs (Attested: Fletcher, 1591, describing what he saw himself).**

> "…they have one at Mosko that walketh naked about the streetes, and inveyeth commonly against the state and government, especially against the Godonoes, that are thought at this time to bee great oppressours of that common wealth."

- **Setup.** Fletcher's own explanation of why the people loved the holy fools: "they are as Pasquils, to note their great mens faults that no man els dare speake of"[34]. _Pasquils_ were the anonymous satires posted on a statue in Rome.

**Surikov.** The current story's detail checks out as Surikov's own account, though I saw it only in search snippets. He told Maximilian Voloshin that he found the model for his yurodivy at a Moscow flea market, "selling cucumbers", and "painted him right on the snow"[37].

## Claims the sources don't support

Each entry gives the current wording, the problem, and what the sources say. Wording that the sources support is not listed. I only checked the seven Fools with jests and the yurodivy story. The stories of Paul, Francis, Pablo de Valladolid, and Calabacillas, and the medieval era intro, were outside this ticket and remain unchecked (the Prado blocked automated reading). These claims about the seven Fools were not checked either: Tarlton's pipe, tabor, and jig; the Yorick reading; that the Tarlton engraving copies an Elizabethan drawing; and the date of the Chantilly chalk drawing (c. 1545). One was checked and holds: Hugo's _Le roi s'amuse_ was banned after one performance[14].

### Stańczyk

1. **"the story goes that at a royal hunt King Sigismund had a bear let out of its cage … The king scolded him for fleeing."** This is better than "the story goes": it is in Bielski's chronicle, a near-contemporary record[1]. In Bielski the bear comes out of a crate (_skrzynia_), it knocked Stańczyk off his horse, and the king laughed at him rather than scolding[1]. Present it as recorded, not as legend, and fix the details.
2. **"Another story has him walking through Kraków with his face bound in a bandage, to settle a bet…"** This has no early source. The earliest version I found is Kraszewski (1839)[2]. Kraszewski cites Górnicki's _Dworzanin_, but the story is not in it[5]. In Kraszewski the bet is with a lord at dinner, not with the king[2]. The current "Another story has him" framing is the right register, but say plainly that it is a later legend.
3. **"c. 1480–1560".** The dates are uncertain. He was alive in 1556 and dead by 1562[7], and Wilska favours c. 1470 to 1557/58[6]. "c. 1480–c. 1560" is defensible but should be marked as approximate at both ends.
4. **"Stańczyk served three kings of Poland"**. The sources secure two, Alexander and Sigismund I; service under Sigismund Augustus is only possible[1][3][6]. Say "served the kings of Poland" or "two, perhaps three, kings".
5. **"less for his clowning than for his nerve"**. The claim is fair, but `GLOSSARY.md` lists "clown" under _Avoid_ for Fools (as in the Tarlton item below). Reword ("less for his foolery").

### Triboulet

6. **Story title "A quarter of an hour before" and "The best-known story says…"** The best-known story is a wandering jest with no 16th-century source. Both 19th-century historians who tell it say it was told of many fools[9][12]. Don't build his story around it, and certainly not the title.
7. **"his jokes had a way of finding the most powerful person in the room"**, the Renaissance intro's **"Triboulet traded barbs with the kings of France"**, and the home teaser's **"Triboulet trading barbs with the kings of France"**. The contemporary record describes a simpleton: Marot calls him "as wise at thirty as the day he was born"[9][10]. His witty retorts are later attributions; Canel says the anecdote collectors "dressed Triboulet in the rags of all the fools and clowns of his century"[9]. The Des Périers stories show a fool who is funny without meaning to be[10]. Recast this as a natural fool whose name later attracted other men's jokes.
8. **"Triboulet served two kings of France, Louis XII and Francis I"** and **"c. 1479–1536"**. Probably more than one man bore the name, and the two were later merged[13]. The death year is given as 1536 or 1537[10][13]. Hedge both.

### Will Somers

9. **"so many auditors about him, and so many 'frauditors'"**. Wilson records three puns: "frauditours … conueiers … deceiuers", in place of auditors, surveyors, and receivers[15]. Somers never said "auditors". Quote Wilson.
10. **"came to Henry VIII's court from Shropshire around 1535"**. 1535 is his first appearance in the accounts[17]. The Shropshire birth is tradition ("born in Shropshire, as some say", Armin 1608)[16]; Wikipedia attributes the arrival story to an 18th-century account[17]. Say "from Shropshire, it was said".
11. **"was still at court for Elizabeth's coronation"**. The only source I found is an uncited sentence on Wikipedia[17]. Unverified.
12. **"Will Somers could cheer Henry VIII when no one else could"** (Renaissance intro). This rests on Armin's 1608 riddles story, labelled Legend above, and on Armin's verse that "Will exilëd sadness many a time"[16]. Soften it ("was remembered for cheering Henry VIII").

### Richard Tarlton

13. **"Once, the story goes, he mocked Sir Walter Raleigh and the Earl of Leicester so mercilessly in her presence that she had him taken away for making her laugh too much."** This merges two different stories. The "take away the knave for making her to laugh so excessively" note is contemporary (1588), and it is about Tarlton fencing with her lapdog[19]. The Raleigh and Leicester story comes from Bohun (1693), and in it the queen frowned, "was so offended", and banned "Tarleton and all her jesters" from her table[12][20]. Tell them as two stories: the attested one (the dog, laughter) and the later one (Raleigh, displeasure).
14. **"the most famous clown in Elizabethan England"**. The facts are right (DNB: comic actor, Queen's Men)[21], but `GLOSSARY.md` lists "clown" under _Avoid_ for Fools. Reword ("comic actor", "the queen's fool").

### Archibald Armstrong

15. **"When Laud's new prayer book set off a riot in Edinburgh in 1637, Archy asked him, 'Who's fool now?' Laud complained to the Privy Council, and in 1638 Archy was stripped of his fool's coat."** This compresses the timeline. The jibe was on 11 March 1638 (1637 in the old calendar) and was about the news from Stirling, months after the 1637 Edinburgh riot. The complaint, the Council order, and the stripping all happened the same day[23].
16. **"Asked to say grace at a dinner Laud attended, Archy prayed…"** This is a legend, first found in 1693, more than 50 years later[24][39]. The earliest version says Archy asked for leave to say grace[24]. Introduce it with "the story goes".
17. **"a courtier wrote home"**. The writer was James Howell, in a letter dated from Madrid in 1623 and printed in 1645[27][28]. Name him; "courtier" overstates his role.
18. **"Richard Gaywood etched him for A Banquet of Jests, a book of jokes sold under his name."** The first edition (1630) is anonymous, signed "Anonimos", and only attributed to Archy[26]. The Gaywood etching (c. 1660, per `paintings.ts`) belongs to a later edition[22]. Say it is from a later edition of the jest book that went under his name.

### Erasmus and Folly

19. **"Folly herself climbs into the pulpit and preaches a sermon in her own praise"**. Erasmus calls the book a _declamatio_ (oration), and Folly "stepped up to speak to this full assembly"[29][30]. The pulpit belongs to Holbein's drawing, not the text. Either say "speech" or credit the pulpit to Holbein.
20. **"he said it took him only a few days"** and the Holbein quip. The first is reported, but I did not read Erasmus's own words[31]. The second rests on a single review[32]. Keep both soft ("is said to"), as the story already does for the quip.

### The yurodivy

21. **"Nikola offered him a slab of raw meat. Ivan protested that it was Lent and he was a Christian. Nikola asked why, then, he drank Christian blood."** This mixes versions. The earliest source (Fletcher, 1591) has the exchange go through a messenger, and in it Nikola speaks of eating "man's flesh"[34]. Horsey has "eater of Christian flesh"[35]. "Drinks Christian blood" belongs to the later _Life_[36]. Quote Fletcher, or say "the story goes" for the hagiographic version.
22. **"Ivan left Pskov largely unharmed."** Fletcher says only that Nikola "saved a great many mens lives"[34], and Horsey that Ivan left[35]. Neither says the town was unharmed. Use Fletcher's wording.

### Home teaser (`src/content/home.ts`)

23. The teaser's Triboulet phrase is covered under Triboulet above. "Stańczyk brooding while the Polish court danced" describes Matejko's painting and is fine as a description of the picture. "the holy fools who rebuked tsars barefoot in the snow" is supported by Fletcher (naked "in the very extremity of winter"; Basil reproving the "olde emperour")[34].
